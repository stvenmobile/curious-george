# 2026-09-13: designing the curiosity-selection mechanism (no code yet)

After Phase 1 confirmed the core hypothesis (see `notebooks/curious_george.ipynb`'s
recorded 5-trial result: `moderate` transfers genuinely, `known` and `noise`
both interfere, severity scaling with how little real structure a topic
has), the natural next question is the one Phase 1 never answered:
given a store of candidate topics, how does Piper actually decide *which
one* to research when idle time is available? This was a long
architecture discussion, deliberately kept to design/prose - nothing was
built yet. Recording the decisions here before starting.

## The starting question

"Go research a topic you're curious about" implies a selection
mechanism. Three shapes were considered: search the internet for
"topics of interest," choose from a list we provide, or use some kind
of curiosity index. Landed on: a persistent memory of candidate topics,
each scored, with selection driven by the scores - not live web search
(no mechanism for that yet) and not a single fixed list (the whole
point is that this should evolve).

## One store, not two

Early instinct was to split "candidates" (noticed, not yet studied)
from "learned" (already in `MemoryStore`) as separate registries. Correct
pushback: real memory doesn't work that way - vague notions and clear
understanding are stored side by side, at different depths, not filed
in different cabinets. Landed on a single store, with items ranked
along a "how well understood" axis rather than sorted into separate
structures. `MemoryItem`'s existing `loss_history` already *is* that
axis; the only gap was that nothing represented "merely noticed, never
studied" - see the cheap/expensive split below for how that gets
resolved without requiring every trivial encounter to become a
permanent memory (explicitly out of scope for now: short-term vs.
long-term memory tiers, eviction policies, etc. - "too early in the
game").

## Three axes per topic, not one

Baseline loss alone can't drive selection - that's the direct,
hard-won lesson from Phase 1. A topic can sit at moderate-to-high loss
either because it's genuinely learnable (`moderate`) or because it's
pure noise (`noise`), and loss alone can't tell those apart. Settled on
three distinct signals, each answering a different question:

1. **Mastery** ("newbie / novice / master") - how well is this already
   understood. Directly readable from loss, calibrated against real
   anchor numbers already measured: newbie ~ noise's 7.8-9.1 nats
   (near the guessing ceiling), novice ~ warbles' pre-study 3.47
   (recognizably novel, not opaque), master ~ warbles' post-study
   0.72-0.86 or known-facts' baseline 1.26 (already had it, or now
   does).
2. **Resonance** - does this align with declared interests. Since
   autonomous taste-formation is out of scope for now, this is
   human-seeded: a small list of declared interest anchors, each
   embedded the same way everything else in `MemoryStore` is embedded,
   and a candidate's resonance is its cosine similarity to the nearest
   anchor - reusing `MemoryStore.query()`'s existing mechanism, not new
   infrastructure. Initial seed list: human curiosity, agentic
   behavior, goal prioritization, memory as a predictor, free will -
   deliberately the subjects this research program is itself about.
3. **Transferability** - the actual Phase 1 signal
   (`generalization_ratio`, the thing that distinguishes real structure
   from noise). Expensive (needs a real LoRA trial against a sibling
   topic), so it can't be computed for every candidate at entry time -
   see the two-tier cost model below.

## Rejected: randomness/temperature as "the unconscious element"

Real discussion point: human curiosity doesn't feel chosen - it feels
like it happens *to* you, not something you deliberately compute.
Tempting conclusion: emulate that with randomness or sampling
temperature. Rejected, deliberately and explicitly - this is close to
exactly the failure mode that started this whole track (the original
"research interesting topics" feature in `piper_assistant` collapsed
into pure temperature-driven noise with no real signal underneath it,
which is why Phase 0/1 exist at all).

The resolution: "feels involuntary" is a fact about *introspective
access*, not a fact about the underlying process being random. Most
real cognition is principled but not introspectable - the feeling of
arbitrariness doesn't require actual randomness. Two mechanisms capture
the same phenomenology without reintroducing noise as the engine:

- **Contextual triggering**: something in current context (a
  conversation, a phrase) gets embedded and matched against stored
  interests via `MemoryStore.query()`. What surfaces wasn't internally
  chosen - it was surfaced *by* something external. This is real
  curiosity-by-ambush.
- **Stochastic sampling over real scores, not instead of them**: a
  softmax/temperature draw among several already-well-scored
  candidates preserves unpredictability while only ever choosing among
  things that earned their way onto the shortlist honestly. Different
  in kind from the original flaw, where temperature *was* the entire
  mechanism.

Also worth keeping: the goal is to emulate curiosity, not necessarily
create it subjectively. The internal scoring can be fully calculated
and fully logged; what gets surfaced to a user doesn't need to show the
arithmetic. Not revealing the calculation produces the same "this came
out of nowhere" effect externally, honestly, without claiming anything
about genuine unconscious process.

## How candidates enter the pipeline

Piper currently has no embodied "world" - her only inputs are
conversation (us) and an LLM's background knowledge. Three population
mechanisms, which turned out to reduce to two mechanisms plus a
scheduling trigger:

1. **Seed population** - the five declared interests, entered
   directly.
2. **Suggestion batches** - content proposed for evaluation, not
   accepted automatically. Framing these as "pseudo-experiential"
   content (crafted to feel stumbled-upon rather than assigned) is a
   presentation choice on top of this same mechanism, not a separate
   pipeline - the closest available substitute for "encountering the
   world" given Piper's actual inputs right now. Authored by us for
   now, same "human-seeded, autonomous later" boundary already drawn
   for resonance.
3. **The idle-time research goal** - not a population mechanism at
   all; this is *when* Piper acts on what's already in the store.
   Revives the original `piper_assistant` architecture, now backed by
   real measurement instead of temperature.

Open, undecided: whether "ranking suggestions" should mean the
mechanical resonance score doing the ranking, or Piper (the LLM itself,
prompted) giving a genuine natural-language reaction as an independent
signal to compare against the mechanical one - parked, not resolved.

## Two-tier cost model

Entry into the store requires only a cheap measurement: one baseline
loss probe (mastery) plus the embedding (resonance) - affordable for a
whole batch of suggestions. The expensive step (a full LoRA trial,
producing the transferability signal) is reserved for deep scoring,
which only runs against whatever a topic needs when Piper is actually
choosing what to spend idle time on - not against every candidate that
merely entered the pipeline.

## Deep scoring, pruning, and archiving

Deep scoring uses the **average of 5 trial runs** - matching Phase 1's
actual validated methodology, not a cheaper single-trial version.
Deliberate choice: Phase 1 measured real run-to-run variance directly
(`moderate`'s ratio ranged 0.126-0.322 across 5 seeds, same topic, same
everything but the training seed) - a prune/keep decision made on a
single trial carries real risk of misjudging a topic, and the decision
was to pay for reliability rather than accept that risk.

Two different fates for leaving the active pipeline, not one:
- **Pruned** - deep score reveals noise (no real transferable
  structure). Removed entirely.
- **Archived** - mastered, and no new information sources are
  currently available to push further. Kept, set aside, not actively
  competing for idle-time selection.

## Rescoring policy

Three cases, resolved cleanly:
1. Existing item, no new research conducted on it since its last score
   - no rescore. The cached score stands.
2. Existing item, new research *was* just conducted on it - rescore.
   This is how the system notices a topic approaching its mastery
   limit (diminishing headroom) and becomes eligible for archiving.
3. New item - must be scored (cheap tier at minimum).

Explicitly parked, not resolved: whether studying one topic could ever
require rescoring *other*, related topics. Under the architecture built
so far (Phase 0 and Phase 1 both reload a fresh base model and discard
the LoRA adapter after every single trial - nothing persists across
trials), there's no mechanical channel through which studying one topic
could affect another's score, so the simple per-item rule above is
sufficient as-is. This only becomes insufficient if/when "real" study
eventually persists into a shared, evolving model rather than staying a
disposable measurement instrument - a genuinely open question about the
eventual deployed architecture, not yet decided, and not needed to
start building the pipeline described above.

## Where this stands

extended `MemoryItem` schema (mastery, resonance, deep-score/
transferability fields, pipeline status: candidate / active / archived
